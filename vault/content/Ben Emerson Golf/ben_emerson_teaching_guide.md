# Ben Emerson Golf — Teaching Guide

Everything Ben Emerson teaches, organised by topic, with the deepest coverage on **sequencing**, **pressure in the feet** and **drills**.

> **How this was built.** YouTube blocked transcript downloads from the machine this was compiled on, so the video transcripts are **not** in here. The content comes from Ben Emerson's own written coaching articles and lesson write-ups on Golf Monthly (about 35 pieces, 2022–2026), which cover the same ground as his videos. His YouTube videos are listed at the end so they can be run through `/process` later. Anything that is my own reading rather than his words is marked **(my note)**.
>
> All directions assume a **right-handed golfer**: lead = left, trail = right.

## 1. Who he is

- PGA Fellow professional, Golf Monthly "Top 50" coach, based in Berkshire, UK (Ben Emerson Golf Performance). Over 20 years of coaching.
- TPI Advanced Golf and Level 3 Power coach, K-Motion/K-VEST 3D biomechanics certified, Swing Catalyst and BodiTrak (pressure/force plate) certified, SuperSpeed Level 3, TrackMan professional.
- Studio kit: GASP 3D force plates, K-VEST 3D motion capture, HackMotion wrist sensor, high-speed cameras, TrackMan, SuperSpeed Force Board, force pedals.
- Co-author with Baz Plummer of the ball-striking book *Strike It Pure*.

## 2. His coaching philosophy

1. **Measure, don't guess.** His motto is "Why guess when you can assess". A new student gets a TPI movement screen, 3D motion capture, pressure/force plate analysis, TrackMan and 2D video before any swing change.
2. **The body sets the swing.** The screen finds physical limits (mobility, balance, hip/shoulder separation) and the swing is built around them rather than forcing a textbook position. Example: if your trail arm cannot rotate back to 90°, a "flying" right elbow is the right answer for you.
3. **Find your "super power".** Most golfers have a physical strength they are not using. In one documented lesson the student's strongest asset was leg power, and the data showed he barely used it.
4. **Sequence over positions.** A bad shot is often not a "swing fault" but a sequencing fault. Fix the order things move in before obsessing over clubface or backswing positions.
5. **Clubface is king** for direction; **strike is king** in the short game; **distance control is king** in putting.
6. **Impact is the only thing the ball understands.** Learn what impact looks like, then your only job is to return there.
7. **Awareness through extremes.** Feel both ends of a spectrum (slow/fast, open/closed, C-posture/S-posture), then settle in the middle.
8. **Slow first.** If you can't do it slowly, you have little chance of doing it at speed.
9. **The best drills often don't look like a golf swing.** Movement drills (jumps, pressure boards) retrain the body faster than swing-plane talk.
10. **Practise like you play.** One ball, full routine, random targets, some pressure.

## 3. Sequencing (the kinematic sequence)

### 3.1 What it is

The kinematic sequence is how energy passes through the body into the club: **lower body → torso/chest → lead arm → club**. Each segment speeds up and then slows down, handing its energy to the next one in a chain reaction.

His order for the change of direction, in his own description: the lower body moves first and the **belt buckle starts moving towards the target**, followed by the chest, then the lead arm, and finally the club.

### 3.2 The whip

- Think of the lower body as the **handle of a whip**. You would never crack a whip by moving the far end first.
- The lower body holds your strongest muscles, so it has to get into play early.
- The part most people miss: segments must **slow down** for the energy to move on. His line from a lesson: *"If you keep moving, the whip never cracks."*

### 3.3 What starts the downswing

- The lower body, and it starts **before the backswing has finished**. The best players shift into the lead foot at the top of the backswing or just before it.
- From the top, good players almost **squat into the ground**. That squat triggers the upper body to rotate, which brings the arms, and then the club.
- The pressure shift into the lead foot is what kicks the sequence off (see section 4). Sequencing and foot pressure are the same lesson seen from two ends.

### 3.4 What it looks like when it goes wrong

| Fault | What's happening in the sequence | Typical result |
|---|---|---|
| Upper body or shoulders fire first | Club is thrown outside the ball-to-target line | Over the top, slice, pulls |
| Club moves first (casting) | Wrist angle released at the top; club is slowing down by the time it reaches the ball | Weak, scooped strikes, lost distance |
| Lower body starts but arms overtake too early | Energy never travels up the chain properly | Push or slice to the right (the "sequencing fault, not a swing fault" case) |
| Hanging back on the trail foot | No early pressure shift, body never gets organised for impact | Thins, blocks, heavy shots |
| Weight moving away from target through impact | Like throwing a ball while stepping backwards | Lost balance, lost power |

He notes that high handicappers most often show the club or upper body moving first.

### 3.5 Release and lag come from sequence

- "Release" is not throwing the hands at the ball. It is letting the stored power go at the right moment, and it happens naturally if what came before was in order.
- In the downswing, feel the club **fall closer to your body** rather than moving away from you. That is what creates lag.
- The trail elbow drops slightly as pressure moves back toward the target, holding its angle early in the downswing, then releases late.
- **Don't force lag.** Manufacturing it makes the swing wooden. Let the body flow in sequence with a good weight transfer.
- Don't hit "at" the ball. Get the sequence right and the club accelerates through impact on its own.

### 3.6 Short game: the sequence is reversed

For chips and pitches he teaches the **opposite** order. The **arms and club start the downswing**, the club should be the fastest-moving thing so the bounce can work, and the body joins in automatically with a gentle hip turn through impact. Let gravity drop the arms and club; don't pull the club down.

## 4. Pressure in the feet and using the ground

### 4.1 The core idea

- The swing works from the ground up. The feet are the only connection to the ground.
- **Ground reaction forces:** the harder and more efficiently you push into the ground, the more the ground gives back. It is about pushing in the **right direction**, not just pushing hard.
- Timing matters because there is almost no time: he puts the whole swing at roughly 1.25 seconds and the downswing at about 0.24 seconds.

### 4.2 Stage by stage

| Stage | Where the pressure should be | His feel or cue |
|---|---|---|
| **Address, irons** | Balanced; sternum directly over the middle of the stance | Sternum forward of centre gives thins and low shots; behind centre gives heavy shots |
| **Address, driver** | Slightly favouring the trail foot | Feet shoulder-width, then step the trail foot one more step away from target; spine tilted slightly away |
| **Address, 3-wood/fairway wood** | 50/50, or slightly lead-side for some players | No leaning back to help it up |
| **Backswing** | Into the **inside** of the trail foot, not the outside edge | "Gently screw the trail foot into the ground"; push toward **4:30** on a clock face; trail foot slightly flared |
| **Late backswing / transition** | Already moving into the lead foot before the backswing finishes | Pressure moves, the body doesn't slide; upper body still completing the turn |
| **Start of downswing** | Ball of the lead foot, under the **big toe and second toe** | "Shift and push into your big toe on the lead foot", toward **10:30** |
| **Downswing** | Lead foot pushing toward 10:30 while the trail foot resists toward 4:30 | A "force couple": two rugby players pushing you in opposite directions to spin you |
| **Impact and finish** | On the lead side, and it stays there | Energy always travelling toward the target; hold a balanced finish |

**(my note)** The articles don't define the clock. The reading that fits everything else he says: you are standing in the middle looking down, 12 o'clock is toward the ball, 3 o'clock is away from the target. So 4:30 is the trail foot pushing away from the target and slightly back toward the heel, and 10:30 is the lead foot pushing toward the target and slightly forward toward the toes, which matches "big toe". The two opposite pushes are what turn the hips toward the target.

### 4.3 The three things to do with your feet

1. **Load the inside of the trail foot.** The amateur mistake is swaying, which rolls pressure to the outside of the trail foot. That costs posture, sequencing and contact. Loading the inside creates resistance to coil against and keeps you centred. His image: on ice, your back foot would slip out behind you. Friction is what you are pushing against.
2. **Shift into the lead foot earlier.** Force plate work shows elite players start moving pressure toward the target before the backswing is finished. Most amateurs stay on the trail side far too long. This early shift is what sequences the downswing, controls low point, and adds speed without swinging harder.
3. **Push in opposite directions to create rotation.** Lead foot toward 10:30, trail foot toward 4:30. Long hitters look explosive because they use the ground better, not because they swing harder.

### 4.4 Pressure cues he uses to fix specific faults

- **Early extension / blocked drives (hips moving toward the ball).**
  - If it happens in the **backswing**: feel like you push into the trail side and into the **trail heel** going back, to stay "sat down".
  - If you stand up in the **downswing**: feel like you push into the **toes of the lead foot**, which lets the lower half get deeper.
  - Upper-body helper: feel the lead shoulder work **down** from the top, which puts you into a squat before unwinding.
  - Check: put an alignment stick in the ground behind you, or film down the line. No daylight should open between you and the line.
- **Hanging back with fairway woods:** imagine your energy always moving toward the target from the top.
- **Limited turn:** lift the lead heel in the backswing (Nicklaus did), but replant it in the downswing.
- **Winter golf:** wider stance for a stable base and fresh spikes for traction, so you can still use ground forces on wet turf.

### 4.5 "Power shift mode" versus a stable base

He is clear that the big pressure shift is for full swings only.

| Shot | Pressure rule |
|---|---|
| Full swing, driver | Full shift: trail inside → early into lead big toe |
| Chips and short pitches | Weight on the lead side at address and it **stays there**; trail foot could almost lift off the ground |
| Bunker from compact sand | 60–70% on the lead side throughout; a big shift only moves the low point |
| Fairway bunker | A touch more weight left, minimal movement, don't dig the feet in much |
| Stack and tilt (as a style) | About 60% on the lead side at address, stay there, bump hips forward and turn |
| Knockdown / punch | Get onto the lead side earlier |

## 5. Drills

### 5.1 Sequencing and foot pressure drills

**1. The 4:30 / 10:30 routine (his "best tip for using the ground")**
- Flare the trail foot slightly.
- Backswing: feel pressure through the inside of the trail foot, pushing toward 4:30.
- Transition: shift and push into the big toe of the lead foot, toward 10:30.
- Can be done without a club at home.

**2. Golf jump drill**
- Make small vertical jumps while turning toward the target side.
- No club needed. Looks silly; teaches the push-and-rotate pattern.
- In the documented lesson, the swings straight after produced higher clubhead speed and tighter dispersion, and largely removed the right miss.

**3. Force pedals / SuperSpeed Force Board**
- Feedback tools under the feet that show where and when you are pushing.
- Goal: push early into the lead foot to start the downswing.

**4. Split tennis ball drill** (he uses it; Golf Monthly's write-up of the drill is by Anders Mankert)
- Cut a tennis ball in half. One half under the **trail heel**, the other under the **ball of the lead foot**.
- Backswing: squash the trail-heel half. Start of downswing: squash the lead-foot half.
- A home substitute for force pedals.

**5. Pause-at-the-top drill** (over the top, slice)
- Swing to the top and pause for a fraction of a second so everything sets and gravity can drop the club onto a better plane.
- Then let the lower half start the downswing and rotate through to a balanced finish.
- Can be used as an on-course rehearsal in the pre-shot routine.

**6. Tee-in-front release drill** (casting, early release)
- Put a tee in the ground on the target side of the ball (he gives roughly two feet).
- Rehearse the post-impact position: hands forward, hips open, extended, clubhead pointing roughly at the tee.
- Hit half-swing punch shots: back to lead arm parallel, through, and stop with the clubhead pointing at the tee.
- Dose: for every five balls, do the drill a few times, then hit one or two full shots.

**7. Throwing analogy drill** (weight transfer)
- Throw a ball down the fairway and notice you step into it. Then try throwing while moving your weight backwards and feel how wrong it is.
- Use that as the feel for the downswing: shift toward the target first, then let chest and arms follow, and stay there.

**8. "Baseball" drill** (slice, over the top)
- Swing the driver like a baseball bat at chest height. You would never swing a bat over the top, and you can see the face closing right in front of you.
- Gradually tilt down into golf posture keeping the same around-the-body motion and forearm roll.
- Use the single word "baseball" as the swing thought. A Golf Monthly writer used it to break 90 for the first time after a 10-shot lesson.

**9. Left-handed (opposite-side) swings and "power whip"** — titles on his channel; content not captured here. See section 9.

### 5.2 Impact and strike drills

**10. Impact rehearsal ("start at impact and work back")**
- Take your setup, then push the hands forward and rotate hips and chest open with pressure moving into the lead side. That is impact.
- Compare it to address: at address the butt of the club points just past your centre; at impact it points past the lead hip.
- Rehearse it with no ball. Then swing to the top and return to it, exaggerating. Start slow, add speed.

**11. Tee peg in front of the ball**
- Tee about two centimetres target-side of the ball. Try to hit the tee.
- Moves your focus in front of the ball and steepens the attack angle for ball-then-turf contact.

**12. Stop-topping drill**
- Topping is often the arms breaking down ("chicken wing"), not the head lifting.
- Rehearse the through-swing with chest and hips turned, elbows feeling squeezed together and coming through together, trail foot slightly off the ground.
- Think "arms tight", not "head down".

### 5.3 Clubface drills

**13. Awareness speed swings**
- At about 25% speed, aiming straight: hit one deliberately right (feel the open face), one deliberately hooked (feel it close), one straight.
- Then keep the two "wrong" swings at 25% and ramp the straight one up a little each time.

**14. Three-ball left / right / middle**
- Aim down the middle. Hit the first as far left as you can, the second as far right, the third straight.

### 5.4 Setup, takeaway and tempo drills

**15. Alignment stick takeaway drill**
- Stick in the ground a few feet behind you, in line with your heels. Treat it as a pane of glass and take the club back without touching it.
- Checkpoints at club-parallel: trail forearm on top of the lead forearm, and the clubhead matching your spine angle.

**16. Posture: S, C, then neutral**
- Club across the shoulders. Hinge and squeeze the shoulders back (S-posture). Then round the back and tuck the head (C-posture). Repeat, then find the flat-backed middle.

**17. Posture: club on the belt line**
- Stand tall, hold a club at both ends just below the belt, push it back into the hips so the bum goes out with a straight back. Add a little knee flex and let the arms hang.

**18. Grip check: index finger only**
- Lead hand on the club with the face square, club raised. Take every finger off except the index finger. If you can still hold the club, the grip is sitting correctly on the pad of the hand.

**19. Trail arm mobility test**
- Stand tall, upper arm out, elbow at a right angle, rotate the forearm back. Reach about 90° and you can keep the elbow tucked at the top. If you only reach about 45°, let the elbow fly and don't force it.

**20. Three-ball tempo drill**
- Three balls teed in a row with driver. Ball one: as slow as you can while still making a full swing. Ball two: as fast as you possibly can. Ball three: the comfortable middle.

**21. Three-ball full-routine practice**
- Only three balls at a time. Step away, do the full pre-shot routine, step in, hit. Repeat.

**22. Alternate-shot drill use**
- One swing with the drill, one normal full swing. The drill is for learning, the swing is for checking.

### 5.5 Short game drills

**23. One-handed (trail hand) chipping**
- Lead hand on your thigh. Light trail-hand grip. Short swings letting gravity drop the club, brushing the grass. Then add a ball, then add the lead hand lightly.
- Fixes both the block release and the flip.

**24. Trail foot on its toes**
- Lead foot flat, trail foot up on the toes. If you shift off the ball you fall over.

**25. Seesaw board**
- Stand on a seesaw board and chip, keeping the target side of the board on the ground the whole time. Any shift shows instantly. He calls this his favourite for players who move off the ball.

**26. Darts**
- Throw six balls to random spots around the green. Play each as it lies to a four-foot circle. Once per session only. Beat your best.

**27. Throw, then copy**
- Throw a ball underarm to a landing marker and watch the flight. Then pick the club that copies that flight. Repeat for a low runner and a high stopper.

**28. Shorter back, accelerate through**
- Shorten the backswing and accelerate to the target, keeping tempo so it doesn't turn into a stab.

**29. "One and two" count**
- "One" back, "and" for the pause in transition, "two" into impact. Grip pressure about 3 out of 10.

### 5.6 Putting drills

**30. Roll the line end over end** — line on the ball aimed at the target; a wobbling line shows an open or closed face.
**31. Ladder drill** — stations made with alignment sticks; stop a ball in each one in turn; fail and start again.
**32. Take the hole away** — putt to a tee peg instead of a hole before the round.
**33. Nine-ball drill** — nine balls at short, medium and long range, random order, keep score.
**34. Gate drill** — a tee either side of the putter head; stroke without touching them.
**35. Fast greens** — three putts from 10–15 ft to a tee, then six different holes with one ball against a par of 12.
**36. Slow greens: two-tee drill** — one tee at the back of the hole, one two feet beyond it; putt to the far tee.

## 6. Everything else he teaches

### Setup

- **Grip.** Neutral is about two to two-and-a-half knuckles on the lead hand, with the trail-hand "V" pointing at the right shoulder. Strong grip (three or four lead knuckles) gives overactive hands and hooks. Weak grip (one knuckle or fewer) leaves the face open: pushes, fades, slices. Slicers can experiment a little stronger and hookers a little weaker, in small steps, on the range.
- **Posture.** Neutral means a flat back and a real hip hinge. The two amateur faults are C-posture (rounded, lazy) and S-posture (arched). A flatter back makes rotation easier.
- **Stance width.** The longer the club, the wider the stance. Driver: shoulder-width plus one extra step with the trail foot. Irons: shoulder-width, widened slightly. Wedges: narrower as the shot gets shorter. Too wide restricts mobility and weight transfer. Sternum over the middle of the stance with every club.
- **Ball position.** Driver inside the lead heel. 3-wood about two balls further back. 7-iron a fraction forward of centre.

### Backswing

- **Takeaway.** A strict "one-piece takeaway" can pull the club inside. The clubhead has the furthest to travel. See drill 15.
- **Trail elbow.** Depends on your mobility. With limited range, a flying elbow is fine (Nicklaus, Couples, Furyk); from there feel the elbow work straight back toward the ball to shallow. Forcing a tucked elbow often causes standing up in the backswing.
- **Length and speed.** Two ways to hit it further: apply force faster (Jon Rahm, short and explosive) or apply it for longer (Dustin Johnson, long swing). So either get stronger or lengthen the backswing. Lifting the lead heel and lifting the arms higher both lengthen it. The biggest hitters are not slow going back, but only add backswing speed once the fundamentals are in place.

### Power: his five clubhead-speed keys

1. Sequencing
2. Backswing length
3. Weight transfer
4. Backswing speed
5. Release at impact, not before

### Faults

- **Over the top / slice:** a sequencing problem. Pause drill, baseball feel, and fix the aim-left habit.
- **Casting:** release drill with the tee; feel the club fall close to the body.
- **Blocks and early extension:** pressure cues in 4.4.
- **Topping:** arms breaking down; drill 12.
- **Scooping irons:** learn impact; drills 10 and 11. Hitting down makes the ball go up.

### Driver versus 3-wood

- **Driver:** widest stance, ball forward, spine tilted away, wide takeaway, lower body starts down so the upper body stays behind the ball.
- **3-wood:** tee it low like a perfect fairway lie, ball two balls back, narrower stance, level or slightly downward strike, weight moving through to the lead side. Don't sweep or lean back. Brushing the turf after the ball is fine. If it still won't fly, you may need more loft (4- or 5-wood) or a fitting.

### Shot-making and trouble

- **Low fade round a tree:** ball back, open stance aimed well left, face at the target, one or two clubs more, grip down, punchy swing, chest over the ball, quiet hands. If not confident, pitch out.
- **Fairway bunker:** one club more for a smooth swing, don't dig in, grip down, a touch more weight left, ball first. High lip means take the loft and just get out.

### Short game

- **Setup:** stance about a clubhead wide, lead foot flared, about five degrees of shaft lean (grip toward the left pocket), ball middle, weight left and staying left.
- **Sequence:** arms and club first (section 3.6).
- **Release:** avoid both the flip and the block; let the club pass the body.
- **Club choice:** with nothing to carry, use a pitching wedge or 9-iron and run it. The 60° is for emergencies.
- **Winter chip:** feet close, hands forward, neutral wrists, rock the shoulders, chest turns to the target.
- **Compact sand:** no wide stance, don't open the face, stand taller, 60–70% left, trust the loft.

### Putting

- Arms hang naturally, feet about shoulder-width, grip in the palms not the fingers, light pressure ("tense muscles are weak and forgetful"), body aimed parallel left of the line.
- Pick a start-line spot a few feet in front of the ball.
- **Fast greens:** shorter backswing, same follow-through, keep the one-two tempo. Don't decelerate.
- **Slow greens:** longer stroke, not a wrist flick. Rehearse four or five feet back from the ball. Imagine rolling the ball by hand to judge stroke length.

### Practice habits (his "never do at the range" list)

1. Arriving without a plan.
2. Machine-gunning 50 balls.
3. Guessing instead of filming. Camera at belt height, down the line, pointing through the hands, slow motion.
4. Getting worked up. The range is a safe place to get things wrong.
5. Neglecting clubface drills.
6. Using the previous shot as distance feedback. Make distance practice random.
7. Only doing drills. Alternate drill and real swing, and vary the speed.

### Mental game and routine

- **Pre-shot routine in two steps.** First, leave the bag back and stand behind the ball to see the whole picture and choose the club. Second, make practice swings **behind** the ball, take a last look, walk in, waggle, one final look, go.
- A single simple swing thought can clear out the negative ones.

### Swing styles he explains

- **Single plane (DeChambeau style):** start from a position closer to impact. Wrists unhinged (ulnar deviation) so lead arm and club form one line, slightly wider stance, lead foot flared more, slight spine tilt away. Trail foot stays planted longer and the club exits down the line.
- **Stack and tilt:** about 60% on the lead side with hands ahead, big turn with the lead shoulder down, stay on the lead side, bump the hips forward and turn. Aims to control low point, power and curvature.

| Block | Time | What |
|---|---|---|
| No club | 3 min | 4:30 / 10:30 routine, then 5–10 golf jumps |
| Feet | 5 min | Split tennis ball: slow swings squashing trail heel, then lead foot |
| Sequence | 5 min | Pause-at-the-top swings, lower body starts |
| Impact | 5 min | Impact rehearsal, then tee-in-front punch shots (five drill reps, one or two full swings) |
| Face | 5 min | Awareness speed swings at 25% |
| Play | 10 min | Three balls at a time with full routine, alternating drill swing and normal swing |

## 8. Quick reference: his one-line cues

- Belt buckle moves to the target first.
- If you keep moving, the whip never cracks.
- Screw the trail foot into the ground: 4:30.
- Big toe of the lead foot: 10:30.
- Pressure moves; the body doesn't slide.
- The club falls closer to you in the downswing.
- Energy always goes toward the target.
- Impact doesn't look like address.
- Arms tight, not head down.
- Chipping: arms and club go first; weight stays left.
- Clubface is king.
- If you can't do it slowly, you can't do it fast.
